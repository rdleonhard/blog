The viceroy butterfly spent about a century in the textbooks as a con artist.

Here's the setup. The monarch eats milkweed as a caterpillar and banks the plant's toxins in its own body, so a bird that eats one spends the afternoon retching and remembers those orange-and-black wings for life. The viceroy, the story went, is harmless. It just wears the monarch's colors and cashes in on somebody else's poison. Then in the nineties, researchers finally got around to feeding viceroys to actual birds, and it turns out the viceroy is foul in its own right. The famous fake was honest the whole time. It took us a hundred years to check.

I bring this up because there are two entirely different ways to wear another creature's colors, and everything depends on which one you're looking at.

The first way is the lie. A harmless species copies the uniform of a poisonous one and freerides on a threat it doesn't carry. Biologists call that Batesian mimicry, after a naturalist who spent eleven years in the Amazon watching insects run the con. It's the cheat from [the warning-colors post](../edgar_i_nothing_am/): the bright little nothing of a bug betting nobody bites to check.

The second way is stranger and better. Sometimes a whole crowd of species converges on the same warning colors, and every single one of them is genuinely poisonous. Muellerian mimicry. Not a con. A co-op. Honest co-branding, a standards body for poison.

Why would honest species dress alike? Because predators learn by eating, and the tuition is paid in bodies. Every naive bird has to be burned once, and if each poisonous species wore its own private uniform, each one would bleed members teaching the same bird the same lesson five separate times. Converge on one pattern and you split the bill. One bad meal and the bird honors the whole ring.

That's the machine. Here's the failure mode.

The ring tolerates liars, but only up to a ratio. When fakes are rare, a bird's average experience of orange-and-black is misery, and the colors hold. When the fakes outnumber the poison, the bird's average experience of those colors is lunch. So it stops flinching. It bites everything. And at that point, everybody gets eaten. The liars, obviously. But the honest too. The poison is still real. The signal about the poison is dead. Past a certain density, mimics don't merely exploit the ring. They burn it down with everyone inside.

You can see where I'm going.

For most of human history, effortful prose was a Muellerian ring. Writing anything decent was expensive. It cost hours in a chair, a mind showing up and staying put, and so the words themselves were warning colors, advertising one simple thing: someone thought this. The cover letter that didn't read like a form meant a person wanted the job enough to sweat over it. The condolence note that found one true sentence meant somebody sat with your grief instead of around it. The care itself was the signal. And every writer who paid full price strengthened the colors for every other writer in the ring. We never signed a treaty. Neither did the butterflies.

Generative text is the Batesian flood.

I want to be careful here, because I love these machines. Anyone who reads this blog knows I think Claude is the most interesting thing to happen in my lifetime. The mimic isn't evil. The bug in borrowed colors isn't evil either; it's playing the board in front of it. But density doesn't care about intent. A few fakes and the ring absorbs them. We are not at a few fakes. Fluent, warm, effortful-looking prose now costs approximately nothing, which means the mimics outnumber the models by a ratio that grows while you read this sentence. And the predators, which in this telling is us, are already adjusting. Watch anyone under thirty read an email. Flat eyes, fast scroll, the standing assumption that nobody is home behind the words until proven otherwise. Hiring managers assume the cover letter is machine-made. Professors assume the essay is. They're usually right, which is exactly the problem, because the bite-everything bird does not spare the honest butterfly.

*So write better. Quality will out.*

No — that misses the shape of it. The mimic writes better. That is what makes it a flood instead of a nuisance. A Roman wax seal never proved much of anything except that copying the signet was expensive; the signal was the cost of the forgery, not the wax. The cost just went to zero.

Which brings the whole thing home in a way I don't love.

This blog cannot prove it isn't a mimic. I typed every word of this, and that claim is worth nothing, because a mimic makes the same claim, in the same font, with the same wry little disclaimers. Any proof I can fit into a paragraph, the flood can fit into a billion paragraphs by morning.

So what's left? The same answer I keep landing on, the one from [the soft boi post](../soft_boi_supremacy/): you cannot fake your way out of a faking crisis. The only signals that survive are the ones that get expensive again. Poison you actually carry. When I sign a filing as a lawyer, that signature is me telling the court it may sanction me personally if what sits above it is false. That signal still works, because there is venom behind it. Showing up in person still works. So does a voice cracking, for now, and so does a decade of being the same person in front of the same witnesses. Skin in the game is the one pigment the flood can't synthesize.

On guard duty in Iraq we ran a sign and countersign, a challenge word and a password, and the standing rule was simple: the moment you suspect the word has leaked, you don't stand there debating it. You change the word.

The written word has leaked. Time to change the word. I don't know yet what the new one is.

*Or maybe that is exactly what a mimic would say.*
