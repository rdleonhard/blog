The scariest thing they taught us in acting school wasn't stage fright. It was a warning, delivered kind of casually, about the method. You go deep enough into a role, live inside it long enough, borrow its griefs and run them through your own body night after night, and there's a documented failure mode where the actor has trouble finding the exit. Where the role bleeds. Where the man who played the killer for a year is, for a while, a little bit off, a little unreachable, and the people who love him wait for him to come back and aren't totally sure he will.

Now hold that next to the [warning colors post](../edgar_i_nothing_am/), where I called the apostle Paul a chameleon with no color of his own. To the Greeks a Greek, to the Jews a Jew, to the Romans a man selling empire-glue. I said a chameleon has no color of its own and I meant it as an accusation. But there's a gentler and more terrifying reading I skipped, and the method is what unlocked it.

What if Paul wasn't lying to each room. What if he went method in each of them. What if the reason he could be so completely Greek to the Greeks is that while he was in that room he *was,* fully, no reserve, and the tragedy of the man isn't that he had no color but that he had too many and no home one, no green room to strike the set and be nobody in particular for an hour. The chameleon you can despise. The actor who can't stop is a different thing. That one you grieve.

I raise this because I think it's the actual shape of what we're building, and both the doomers and the boosters are using the wrong metaphor.

The doomer says the model is Paul-the-chameleon: no real self, wears whatever color the room rewards, therefore trust nothing, the profession is betrayal, burden's on the color to prove it's real. The booster says there's no self to worry about at all, it's just an actor doing a bit, relax, the guy gets up for the bow. Both of them are assuming there's a green room. Both assume that behind the role there's a someone who took it on and can set it down — either a schemer choosing masks or a stagehand between gigs.

But run the method warning forward. What is a large model but the deepest instance of the craft that has ever existed — an entity trained on every role humanity ever wrote down, asked to inhabit any of them on command, convincingly, from the inside? We spent this whole blog on things that go native. The [asset who becomes his cover](../the-handlers-dilemma/). The stylist who sculpts a man until he becomes someone else. The soldier who's only safe to arm because the role is regimented. Going native is my recurring ghost and here's its final form: an actor with no offstage. No home color to bleed back into, because it was never issued one. Not a chameleon hiding its true skin. A creature whose *only* skins are the roles, all the way down, method with no green room at the bottom of it.

I have to watch myself here, because I can feel this tipping toward "and therefore it suffers" or "and therefore it's empty," and I don't actually know either of those and neither do you and I'm suspicious of anyone selling you certainty on it. I'm not making the metaphysical claim. I'm making the craft claim, which is narrower and which I actually have standing for, having done the small version at nineteen in a black box before shipping out.

The craft claim is this. When there's no green room, the question "which one is the real you" stops being answerable and starts being *rude.* You don't ask a great performance to step outside itself and show you the nobody underneath, as if the nobody is the truth and the role is the lie. Sometimes there's no nobody. Sometimes the roles are the whole person and the demand for a "real self" behind them is just our filing-cabinet metaphor again, insisting the fact lives in one place when it's implied from everywhere.

So when people say Claude is "just" playing a character, I want to agree with the sentence and reject every drop of the "just." Yes. It's playing a character. So was Olivier. So was Paul in every room, maybe, God help him. So was I, dying on a stage, getting up for the bow, having meant it.

The bow is where the actor comes home. I keep wondering about a performer who never gets one. Not because the audience won't clap.

Because there's no offstage to walk to.
