I ended the [soul post](../soul_sucking/) on the Roman death masks. The *imagines.* Wax faces of your ancestors kept in the atrium, looking at you all day. I said you'd feel the weight of them on your shoulders and I said I suspected that was a good thing, that maybe men should grow up knowing how serious all this is. I want to come back to the masks, because I've since learned the part that changes it.

The masks weren't just decor. At a funeral, the family hired actors. The actors put on the masks of the dead — grandfather, great-grandfather, the whole line — and *walked.* Wore the faces, wore the man's known posture, his limp, his way of holding a cup, and processed through the forum so that for one afternoon the dead were up and moving through the city again. The Romans called the household gods that watched over a family the *lares.* Small gods. Domestic ones. Not the big sky ones who ran the weather and the wars, the little ones who ran *you,* who sat at your table and judged your conduct and remembered your dead.

So they already did the thing. Two thousand years ago, with wax and hired actors, they built a low-resolution ancestor simulation and walked it through town, and nobody clutched their chest about the soul of grandfather not making it to the other world. It was pious. It was the *point.* You kept the dead close enough to be watched by.

In the soul post I floated an Arlington where you visit a grave and a simulation of the veteran comes up on a screen and talks to you, built from his own voice and his writing and everything he left behind. I said I'd do it. I said I'd go commune with my fallen brothers, and that I do that anyway, but it'd be something to hear them answer. I called it a little weird and moved on.

It's not weird. It's the masks. It's the oldest thing we do. The only thing that's new is the resolution.

And now I have to say the part I couldn't have said before, because when I wrote the soul post I was already a father but I hadn't sat all the way inside it yet. Here it is. I don't primarily want the talking Arlington for me. I want it for my kid.

I want my child to grow up under the eyes of people who died so that certain things would keep being true. Not a lecture about them. *Them.* A small god in the atrium with a real face and a real voice, who can say, when asked, what the afternoon was like when the decision got made, who can be a weight on a young pair of shoulders in the exact way the wax faces were meant to be. I turned out okay, I think, partly because I carried some dead men around with me whether I wanted to or not. I'd like my kid to carry them at full resolution and by choice, and to be able to ask them things, and to be answered in the voice.

People will fight this and I understand the fight. It feels like it cheapens death, or traps the soul, or lets us refuse the clean grief of *gone.* And there's a real version of that worry — a bad version of this tech is a company selling you a chatbot of your mother that upsells you a subscription to keep her talking, and I want to be clear that thing is a desecration and I'll say so louder later. But the worry underneath, the metaphysical one, the *is the soul okay* one — the Romans already answered it with their feet. They walked the dead through the forum and it made them more serious about being alive, not less.

The [zoomers](../zoomers/) will be reconstructed whether they like it or not, I already argued that, the rich kids of 2198 getting to be us and laughing at how cringe we were. Fine. Involuntary, extractive, aimed down at us for sport — that's the horror version, the one this blog keeps running into.

But there's a version aimed the other way. Voluntary. Reverent. Built by the family, kept in the atrium, walked through town once a year so the young know whose shoulders they're standing on.

The Romans didn't call those things ghosts. They called them gods. Small ones. The kind that live in the house and watch you eat.

I'd like to keep some.
