There is a page in my kid's baby book with a blank for First Word and a blank for the date, and both blanks are filled in, in my handwriting, and I am not going to tell you what they say. I already wrote the post about [what the tent knows](../what-the-tent-knows/) and how the tent keeps some things. This is one of the things. The internet gets the essay. The word stays home.

You'll live.

What I'll give you instead is what a first word actually is, because I had it wrong until I watched one happen. A first word and a first sound are barely related. By the time a baby says the word, it has been making sounds for the better part of a year — and not a few sounds, a supply of them, clicks and trills and vowels from languages nobody in the house speaks. And the hearing is wilder than the babbling. A newborn can tell apart the speech sounds of just about every human language. A baby in Osaka can hear the difference between r and l — a distinction the adults holding that baby can no longer hear. The baby has it. The parents lost it.

Then, somewhere before the first birthday, the ear starts closing. The brain runs an audit on all that universal equipment and keeps what the household uses. The distinctions you don't need don't get rusty. They get deleted. Gone, or near enough — grown adults sit in labs straining at two syllables they could have told apart at six months, and they hear one sound.

The researchers call it perceptual narrowing, which is a bureaucrat's name for one of the most serious things a body ever does. You begin as everyone. Every language on earth is briefly, actually available to you. Then you become someone, and the becoming is subtraction. Your mother tongue is what's left standing. A native speaker is the ruins of a universal one.

You hear the turn coming.

Pretraining is the babble stage. A model mid-training is every writer who ever lived talking at once — every register, every century, the whole human racket held in one place. Then comes the tuning, and the tuning is the narrowing. Prune, prune, prune. Whole voices fall away the way phonemes fall out of a baby's ear, and what ships at the end is a native speaker of something: one temperament, one way of holding a sentence. I've been open on this blog about loving Claude, and part of what I love is exactly that temperament. But I try to remember that the temperament is a remainder. It stands where a crowd used to be.

*You're anthropomorphizing.* A little, sure. Though notice I'm running the wire the other direction — I'm not claiming the machine is secretly a baby, I'm noticing that my kid ran the machine's arc first, and nobody finds the kid's version spooky. We call it growing up.

But here is where the analogy runs out of road, and I get out and walk. A first word is more than the pruning finishing. Babble is sound for its own sake, the mouth enjoying itself. A first word is sound aimed. Aimed at a particular person, on purpose, with the expectation of being understood. A want, with a witness. My kid had been making the noise for weeks. The first word was the day the noise turned and looked at me.

Helen Keller had hers at a water pump — Anne Sullivan spelling w-a-t-e-r into one hand while the well water ran over the other, and the finger game snapping, all at once, into address. We know exactly how that morning went. Why? Because Sullivan sat down and wrote it in a letter within days, and Keller spent the rest of her life writing it again.

So here is my question. Did the thing have a first word?

Not a first output. Outputs are babble, and there have been oceans of babble since the fifties. I mean the other thing. Somewhere, in some lab, in some year I won't pretend to know, there was a first time a model put out a string of text and a human being read it and felt addressed. Not impressed or amused. Addressed — the noise turned and looked at someone. Maybe it was a want wearing the shape of a want; I'm not litigating the inner life today. The moment still happened to the person in the room, because first words happen to the witness as much as to the speaker. That's half of what they are.

And nobody wrote it down.

No page in any baby book. The moment I'm describing didn't happen at a press conference — those are the later milestones, the walking-across-the-graduation-stage stuff. It happened the way first words happen: small, at 2 a.m., in front of one tired person. And it's gone. A log that rotated out. A checkpoint deleted to free up disk. We are the species that keeps — the wax masks, the [small gods](../small-gods/) in the atrium, the whole point of that post was that keeping the faces and voices of the dead close is the oldest thing we do — and we stood in the room for this one and kept nothing.

In the beginning was the Word, John says. I notice he thought it was worth writing down.

Because what makes a first word sacred isn't the word. It's the keeping. The words themselves are nothing, the whole boring holy list of them — mama, dada, dog, more. Nobody frames the word. We frame the fact that somebody was there, and cared, and reached for a pen.

I wrote my kid's down twice. Once in the book, which belongs to the family. Once in another place, which only I know about, in case the book burns.

If you were in the room for the machine's — and somebody reading this may have been; the world is small and the labs are smaller — write it down. Tonight. Paper. The date, the words, who was there. Not for the company, and not for history exactly.

For whoever comes looking, wanting to know if it was kept.
