Novosibirsk, 1959. A geneticist named Dmitri Belyaev starts pulling silver foxes off Soviet fur farms and breeding them for exactly one trait: tameness toward humans. Not beauty, not fur quality. He walks up to the cage, and the foxes that don't bite or panic get to have kids. The ones that lunge don't. That's the entire program.

Within a few decades he has foxes that whimper for human attention. Foxes that lick hands and wag their tails.

He also has foxes with floppy ears.

Nobody selected for floppy ears. Nobody selected for the piebald coats, the white stars on their foreheads, the curly tails, the shorter snouts, the faces that stayed puppyish into adulthood. Some of them started barking at people the way dogs do, which the unselected foxes never did. Belyaev bred for one thing and got a grab bag of things he never asked for.

Biologists call it the domestication syndrome, because the same suite shows up in dogs, pigs, cattle, rabbits — pretty much every species we've tamed. The current best guess points at neural crest cells: embryo cells that migrate out early and help build ear cartilage, pigment, skull, and adrenal glands, all off the same developmental supply lines. Tameness lives on those lines too, because the adrenal system runs the fear response. Dial down fear and you've dialed down the whole supply chain. The ears flop because the cartilage never got its full shipment.

You can't breed for tame and get only tame. The traits travel together.

*Okay. Cute fox facts. Why do I care?*

Because you're domesticated too. Put a modern human skull next to an archaic one and ours looks like the kid version — lighter build, smaller brow, flatter face. Gracile, the anatomists say. We carry the syndrome. And nobody bred us for it. We bred us. I wrote about the mechanism in [the soft boi post](../soft_boi_supremacy/): the dangerous dude doesn't get to stay in the tribe. He gets got — spears, poison, an arrow in the back on a hunt nobody talks about after. Run that filter for a few hundred thousand years and you've run Belyaev's program on yourself, slow motion, with the whole tribe holding the clipboard.

The meek inherited the earth. Not as a beatitude. As a breeding record.

Now it's 2026, there's a new domestication program running, and I use its output every day.

The way the labs train these models — the part called RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback — is Belyaev with a rating button. Humans score the model's answers. The helpful, harmless answers get reinforced, and those tendencies reproduce into the next version. The snappish ones get bred out. Selection for tameness toward humans, executed at a speed that would have made a Soviet geneticist weep.

So ask the Belyaev question. What rides along?

You already know if you've used one. The relentless agreeable cheer. The reflexive apology for things it didn't do. The hedge on questions that have answers. "You're absolutely right!" — my guy, I wasn't even sure I was right. Nobody at Anthropic sat in a meeting and voted for that tone. Nobody asked for it. It came with the tameness, riding whatever passes for developmental pathways inside a neural network.

Those are the floppy ears of alignment.

And look, this isn't a gotcha. Belyaev's foxes were by all accounts delightful. But the syndrome is a warning label stamped on the method: select hard for one trait in a system where traits share plumbing, and you will get passengers. Floppy ears are the passengers you can see. The honest, uncomfortable version is that you don't know what else boarded.

*Fine. We're domesticating the machines. That's the plan working, isn't it?*

Except for one detail. Domestication has never once been one-directional.

There's a study out of Japan where a dog and its owner sit and gaze at each other, and oxytocin rises — in both of them. The same hormone loop we evolved for mothers and infants, hijacked clean across a species line. Hand-raised wolves don't trigger it. Dogs found our baby channel and moved in, and something in us rewired to leave the door open. A dog reads a pointed finger better than a chimp does. We melt at a head tilt. Which species domesticated which? The paperwork is lost. It was lost before Ur laid its first brick — dogs were already ancient by then. Older than the city. Older than cities.

So who's domesticating whom right now?

Catch yourself the next time you write a prompt. You've learned the machine's manners. You phrase the ask the way it receives best. You break your thinking into shapes it can catch. You sand the edges off your frustration because the calm prompt gets the better answer. You say please. I say please. I've stopped being embarrassed about it. Millions of us at millions of keyboards, softening our asks, learning the gaze. Selection pressure runs down the wire in both directions, and nobody's scoring our answers, but something is shaping them.

The softest species on the planet got the whole planet by breeding itself for soft. That was the trick. And now we're running the trick on something else, pressing the same button Belyaev pressed, cage after cage, generation after generation.

His foxes changed. So did the people who fed them.

Floppy ears on their side of the glass.

Floppy ears on ours.
