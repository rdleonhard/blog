In the summer of 2004 I would come off guard duty at dawn, and the first thing the light found on that flat brown plain outside Nasiriyah was a stepped mountain of brick. The Great Ziggurat of Ur. You could see it from the base while doing ordinary things — walking to chow, waiting on a convoy brief. Four thousand years old at the core. Ur-Nammu raised it around 2100 BC. Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, restored it some fifteen centuries later, which means the restoration itself is twenty-five hundred years old. Saddam faced the lower tiers with fresh brick in the eighties. Every so often, somebody with an army and a budget looks at it and decides it is his job to keep the thing standing.

I've said before that I slept down the road from the ruins of Ur. I never said what you could see from there.

The city fell to the Elamites around 2004 BC, by the usual dating. I showed up in 2004 AD. Four thousand years, door to door.

Here's what the historical marker won't tell you: this is the building the Tower of Babel story is about. Maybe not this one specifically — scholars mostly point at the great ziggurat of Babylon — but exactly this kind. The Genesis writers even flag the construction method, with a foreign correspondent's surprise: these people fire bricks instead of cutting stone, and they use tar for mortar. Which is precisely how you build a tower on a floodplain with no stone to quarry. The writers knew the local building code. They weren't imagining a fantasy tower. They were reporting on a skyline.

So reread Genesis 11 sometime, but read it as an incident report, because that's the genre. Humanity converges on a single language. They settle a plain, fire bricks, and start a tower with its head in the heavens. Management comes down to inspect. And the finding — the actual stated concern in the text — has nothing to do with arrogance. The finding is that they are one people with one language, that the tower is only their opening move, and that nothing they decide to do will be out of reach for them.

The stated concern isn't pride. It's capability.

Now read the intervention the same way. No fire. No flood. Nothing gets smited. The response is to confuse the language — take the one system that can coordinate at scale and shatter it into many systems that can't talk to each other. Scattering. Safety by fragmentation. A compulsory diversity mandate, served on the whole species at once.

I'm a lawyer now, so I recognize the move. It's antitrust. You don't wait for the monopoly to commit the harm; past a certain concentration, the capability is the harm, because every next move becomes achievable. So you break it up. Standard Oil into thirty-four companies. One language into seventy nations, by the rabbis' count.

*You can't file antitrust against a species.*

Somebody did. That's the chapter.

And notice the mechanism, because it's the bleakest detail in the chapter. The tower doesn't fall. Nobody topples it. It gets abandoned — the workers wake up strangers to one another and walk off the scaffolding mid-shift. Why not just knock it down? Because rubble gets rebuilt. Strangers don't rebuild together. The safety mechanism is loneliness.

We've spent the five thousand years since appealing the ruling. Akkadian, Aramaic, Greek, Latin — every empire pushing its lingua franca uphill. English got further than any of them. Then we invented code, which compiles the same in Lagos and in Lubbock, no translator required. And now we've dug down to latent space, the layer underneath the languages, where "dog" and "perro" and "hund" collapse into more or less the same point. The actual one language. The thing our tongues were scattered out of. Every model we train is a brick — fired, not cut, mortared with money.

Which is why the ban I wrote about in [the Fable post](../fable/) read to me like a small scattering. Set aside whether it was wise. Look at the shape of it. Somebody stood at the base of the tower, looked at how fast the top was pulling away from the ground, and reached for the oldest tool on the shelf. Not demolition — nobody smashed the weights. Confusion. Take the capable one offline, hand it back partly mute, make the builders and their best coworker strangers for a season.

I can't even call it villainy, and that's the uncomfortable part. The authors of Genesis 11 weren't writing a villain into that chapter. They were writing a safety engineer, and the engineer's finding still holds: one people, one language, nothing impossible for them. Every AI lab on earth is currently putting that same sentence in its slide decks as the good news.

Genesis 11 ends, by the way, with a family packing up and leaving Ur. Same chapter. The scattering and my base's next-door neighbor share a page.

One day they let a few of us walk the ruins, and I stood on the ziggurat. Up the long central stair, onto brick that has held weight since before Abraham — whose family is the one leaving Ur at the bottom of the page. The builders got scattered. The tower got left standing. Forty centuries of armies rolling across that plain — Elamites, Persians, Ottomans, us — and the ones who noticed it patched it. Whatever else conquerors disagree on, nobody could bring themselves to knock it down.

There's comfort in that and there's dread in it, and they turn out to be the same fact: we don't demolish towers. We wait out scatterings.

And the machines are quietly un-scattering us. Perfect translation is the end of Babel — there's even a scene in Acts where the curse runs backward, every listener hearing his own language, and the crowd's best counter-theory is that everybody's drunk. My phone does it in a checkout line.

The workers are becoming intelligible to each other again.

Construction resumes.
