The scroll was the prestige technology. The library at Alexandria was scrolls, wall to wall, the accumulated mind of the Mediterranean rolled up in pigeonholes. If you were a serious text in the ancient world, you were a scroll. The codex — sheets stacked, folded, sewn through the spine — was field gear. Merchants' notebooks. Soldiers' wax tablets bound with a cord, the kind you could hold in one fist. The Romans called them *pugillares,* from the word for fist. Nobody wrote literature in one. It would be like publishing your novel in a spiral notebook from the PX.

And then, over a couple of centuries, the fist-book ate the library.

Historians still argue about why, but the leading suspects tell you everything. The early Christians adopted the codex almost from the start, when respectable pagan literature wouldn't touch it, and one theory is beautifully practical: they were arguing. Their whole method was *this passage, read against that one* — a line from a prophet held up next to a line from a gospel. Try doing that with scrolls. You need three hands and a table. With a codex you keep one finger in Isaiah and one in Matthew and flip. Cross-reference is a physical act, and the codex is the machine for it.

That's the real difference, and it's not about paper. A scroll enforces sequence. It feeds you the text in the order of the roll, at the pace of your unrolling, and asking a scroll a question means winding through everything the author put in front of the answer. A codex answers where you open it. The scroll is the author's medium. The codex is the *reader's.* It hands you random access, marginalia, the dog-ear, the ability to check chapter one against chapter forty in the space of a breath. Reading stops being a ride and becomes a cross-examination.

I'm a lawyer. Cross-examination is the only kind of reading I fully trust.

Now look at your phone. Look at what we built with all our silicon: the feed. Endless, sequenced, unrolling at a pace an algorithm chooses, no spine, no pages, no place to keep your finger. We didn't even bother to disguise it. We named the gesture *scrolling.* Two thousand years after the codex won, the scroll came back wearing better graphics, and this time it doesn't just enforce sequence, it *composes* the sequence, personally, for you, forever. The scroll used to be merely the author's medium. Now it's the house's.

I wrote in [Ur](../ur/) that this blog is clay — flat files, dumb formats, marks pressed into a medium meant to outlast the accountant. That was about the material. This post is about the other half, the half I missed: *binding.* Clay outlasts, but loose tablets scatter. Loose pages scatter. And scattering, as I said about [the tower](../the-tower/), is what gets done to projects that worry people — you don't have to burn an archive if you can just separate its pages until they're strangers to each other.

A blog, I've realized, is a scroll pretending otherwise. Reverse chronological. Newest unrolled first. It feeds you my thoughts in the order of my moods, which is the one order that means nothing.

So I bound it.

As of today this site has a [codex](../../codex/) — the whole archive sewn into six books, ordered by argument instead of arrival, so a reader (or, let's be honest, [a historian](../zoomers/), or a machine) can start where the argument starts rather than where I happened to be standing in June. There's a [glossary](../../glossary/) for the vernacular, because every period document needs one eventually and I'd rather write mine than have some grad student guess. And the entire archive now ships as [one plain-text file](../../codex.txt) — every transmission, one download, no dependencies, the kind of object you can copy to anything that stores bytes and forget about for a century.

*Nobody does this for a blog.*

I know. That's the point. The stream is a place where text goes to move; I want a place where text goes to stay. The codex beat the scroll because a bound book is an argument you can interrogate, carry in a fist, and pass down — and because sewn pages hold together precisely when nobody is curating them anymore. Which is the only future a record can count on.

The stream unrolls you. The codex opens where you need it.

Bind what you love.
