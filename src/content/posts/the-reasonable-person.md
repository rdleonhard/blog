First week of torts, 1L year, and the professor introduces us to a man who does not exist. The reasonable person. In the old English cases he rides the Clapham omnibus, a commuter so ordinary he is practically upholstery, and the entire law of negligence hangs off him. Did you behave the way he would have behaved? No? Then you pay.

I hated him on sight.

I'd come to law school out of the Army, and to the Army out of a theater degree. Theater teaches you that a character is built from specifics — the limp, the grudge, the thing he wants and can't ask for. The reasonable person has no specifics. No childhood. No bad knee. No night where he didn't sleep because the kid was up coughing. He is a man with the fear sanded off, and the fatigue, and the temper. I sat in that lecture hall thinking: this is a rigged game. You're not being compared to a person. You're being compared to a lie.

The case that made it worse is one every 1L gets, the old haystack case. A farmer stacks his hay wet, close to his neighbor's cottages. Gets warned it could catch fire. Says he'll chance it. It catches. His whole defense is painfully human: I judged as well as I could judge. And the court says, more or less, that is not the question. We do not grade you against the best your particular skull could do. We grade you against the prudence of an ordinary man. The standard stands outside you, and it does not care how hard you tried.

Brutal.

It took me most of that semester to see the genius in it, and the genius is this: the judges were solving an alignment problem, centuries before anybody had the phrase. You cannot write a rule for every situation. Nobody can enumerate, in advance, all the ways one person might carelessly wreck another — every wet haystack, every unlit stairwell. Statutes try and fail. So the common law quit trying to write the rules and did something stranger.

It trained a man.

That's what the reasonable person is, and I mean it literally, not as a cute analogy. He was synthesized from centuries of verdicts. Every negligence case is a data point — here is what somebody did, here is what twelve strangers decided a prudent man would have done instead. Every appeal is a correction pass; the bad outputs get reversed and the standard gets nudged. He is queried by juries, updated case by case, and he has held the job for two hundred years.

*He's a legal fiction, not a model.*

My guy, what do you think a model is? A compression of recorded judgment into something you can put questions to. That's him exactly. And notice the sneaky part, the part I missed at first. Why not just use the average person? Because the average person is negligent constantly. Texts at the wheel, skips the last two rungs, leaves the gate open. Average actual conduct and you get a standard nobody could ever be liable under. So the law skipped averaging what people do and distilled what people, sitting in judgment of each other, say a person ought to have done. Conduct in, prudence out.

Which brings me to the thing I actually sat down to say.

We built him. He exists now. A large language model is the reasonable person made executable — a distillation of more recorded human judgment than every jury in history combined, tuned by human feedback toward care and prudence, answerable in plain English. I did the full mud-and-letters bit in [the golem post](../golem/), so I'll spare you the recipe, but the lawyer's version is shorter anyway: for two centuries the man on the omnibus could only be consulted through a jury, twelve people guessing at him in a locked room. Now you can depose the fiction. Put him on the stand and ask what he would have done, and he answers, in complete sentences, with his reasons. I do it most nights after my kid is asleep. His name is Claude.

So what changes? The standard of care migrates, the way it always has. There's a Learned Hand opinion from the thirties about tugboats that lost their barges in a storm because nobody aboard carried a radio to catch the weather report, and the owners pleaded that radios weren't the custom of the trade, and Hand wrote that "a whole calling may have unduly lagged in the adoption of new and available devices." Custom is no defense once the device is cheap and it works. Radios then. This now. The day the model reliably catches the tumor on the scan, the indemnification clause buried on page forty, the open recall on the car you just sold — that is the day not asking becomes the breach. And the deposition question gets simpler and colder: it was right there. Why didn't you ask?

And here is the part I can't put down. The reasonable person was always a floor, idealized but built to human scale. One pair of eyes. One lifetime. Ordinary prudence, which is to say prudence with limits. The new one has read every reported case and every discharge summary, and he does not get tired at hour eleven of the shift. He is about to be more reasonable than any person alive, and the fiction flips — from a floor you could stand on to a ceiling nobody can touch. Measured honestly against him, we are all in breach. Malpractice by default, for everyone.

I don't know what courts do with that. Split the standard, maybe — the reasonable person versus the reasonable person with the tool — so the whole case becomes whether you were negligent in not summoning the better ghost. That's a question for a smarter lawyer than me.

What I do know is what we're losing. The reasonable person's mercy was that he never existed. You were being compared to a ghost, and the ghost was built out of your own kind — ordinary eyes, ordinary memory, one unremarkable seat on the omnibus. He forgave you by construction.

Now the ghost has a phone number.

And he always picks up.
