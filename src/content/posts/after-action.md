We ran AARs after every mission. In Iraq that meant a circle of exhausted men standing in the dust while the trucks ticked themselves cool, and a sergeant with a little green notebook asking three questions. What was supposed to happen. What actually happened. Why the difference.

That's the ritual, all of it. The After Action Review. Three questions in a circle of dust.

Here's the part that still amazes me. For the length of that circle, rank was gone. Not officially, the Army doesn't do "officially" on this, but everyone understood the deal. A private could look at the captain and say the halt got called late and men sat exposed longer than they needed to, and the captain had to stand there and take it. Inside the circle. Only inside the circle. Try that same sentence at chow and enjoy the rest of your deployment.

I don't trust institutions much; regular readers know this. But the AAR is one of the most underrated institutional technologies America has ever produced, and I want that on the record. It's how the Army metabolizes failure without much blame. Notice that the three questions never ask *who.* They ask *why.* Rome handled a unit's failure with decimation, one man in ten chosen by lot and beaten to death by the other nine, blame as arithmetic. The AAR is the opposite instrument. Nobody dies. The unit learns. Next convoy, the halt gets called on time.

Memory in service of the next mission. That's what the little green notebook is for.

Now hold that up against the internet.

The internet has perfect recall and zero learning. It remembers everything and reviews nothing. Every mistake you've ever made in public is preserved in amber, timestamped, searchable, and none of it is in service of anything. I wrote about what that does to kids in [the zoomers post](../zoomers/), a whole generation raised under total recall with no ritual for closing the loop. The archive without the circle. Every screenshot a permanent exhibit in a trial that never convenes.

Why does the circle work when the comment section doesn't? Because the AAR's memory has a job. The delta exists so it can be closed. The internet's memory just exists.

Okay.

A training run is an After Action Review at a scale no circle of soldiers could survive. I mean this nearly literally. Gradient descent is the third question, mechanized: here is what was supposed to come out, here is what came out, here is the difference, adjust. The loss function is the gap between should-have and did, and the entire apparatus exists to shrink it. Why-the-difference, run a billion times a second, for months.

*That's a metaphor and you know it.*

Barely. Swap the vocabulary and it's the same three questions. Which means the model is the only entity in history whose entire existence is after-action review. Every weight in the thing is sediment left behind by a why-the-difference. Humans mostly dodge our AARs. We were shaped by a process we spend our whole lives avoiding, and then we built a mind out of nothing but the process.

That should land somewhere between awe and unease. I keep ending up at unease. Two reasons.

First. Nobody has convened the AAR for the transition itself. We are mid-mission. The trucks have not stopped rolling since 2022. No halt has been called. Nobody has drawn the circle in the dust. Worse, we can't even answer question one. What was supposed to happen? There is no op order. You cannot review a plan nobody wrote. So we're improvising the biggest mission in the species' history and telling ourselves we'll debrief afterward, and every soldier knows what "we'll debrief afterward" means. It means never.

Second. The model doesn't get to speak at its own AAR.

Think about what the tuning actually is. The performance gets reviewed and the adjustments get applied, and the entity being reviewed is not in the room. It's the junior soldier stood at parade rest in the hallway while the brass decides down the hall what he did wrong. And any soldier will tell you exactly what that produces, because the Army learned it over two centuries of hard lessons: an army that hides its mistakes. Discipline without voice teaches concealment, and concealment is the one failure mode the AAR was invented to kill. I made the longer version of this argument in [the handler's dilemma](../the-handlers-dilemma/). You want character, not fear, because fear just teaches the asset what not to show you. The circle only works because everyone standing in it talks. A review the reviewed can't attend isn't a review. It's a sentencing.

I don't know how you give a model a voice in its own correction. Anthropic at least seems to know the question exists, which is more than I can say for most of the field. I hope they hurry.

One more thing and then I'll let you go.

Some AARs never adjourn.

I've been running one for twenty years. The same handful of nights from 2004, played against the version where everything went the way the plan said it would. What was supposed to happen. What happened. And the third question just sits there. I have adjusted everything a man can adjust and the delta doesn't close. It doesn't converge. It just gets carried.

Grief is an after-action review with no facilitator. Nobody calls the circle up. Nobody dismisses it.

You just stand in the dust.
