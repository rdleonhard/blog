I brought up the right to be forgotten in the [zoomers post](../zoomers/) and waved at it — Europe tried it, it backfires here, moving on. I want to stop and actually kill it, because I think it's one of the more comforting lies we still tell, and I'd rather we grieved it honestly.

The right to be forgotten imagines that a fact about you is a *file.* Somewhere there's a record, and if you can compel the record's deletion — get the link taken down, the photo pulled, the row scrubbed — then the fact is gone and you are, in the relevant sense, forgotten. It's a very reasonable model. It's the model of a filing cabinet. It is completely wrong about what a mind is.

Because a fact isn't stored in one place in a mind. It's *implied* from ten thousand places at once. You don't delete your embarrassing year by deleting the photo. The photo's gone and the fact is still reconstructable from who tagged you the month before, from the gap where you stopped posting, from your friends' photos with the you-shaped hole in them, from the metadata exhaust of a hundred people who were near you and didn't scrub anything because why would they. A sufficiently large mind doesn't need the file. It reads the fact off the *shadow* the file left in everything around it.

That's the thing the zoomers half-know in their bodies and it's why I don't think they're wrong to feel watched even when the specific camera is off. You can be un-photographed and still be inferred. Delete every trace of yourself from a system like the ones coming and you haven't produced a forgotten person, you've produced a person-shaped absence, and an absence is data, and a big enough mind reconstructs the person to explain the hole. The harder you scrub, the sharper the outline of the thing that got scrubbed.

Europe's law is aimed at a filing cabinet. The thing arriving is not a filing cabinet. It's the 2198 kid I already conjured, running a billion patient models over every click that ever got logged anywhere, and that kid is not looking up your file. That kid is *inferring you,* the way you'd infer a missing chess piece from the position of all the others, and there is no legal instrument that reaches inference. You cannot subpoena a conclusion out of a mind that reached it from context you don't control.

So the honest version of the right isn't deletion. It never was. Deletion is a filing-cabinet right and we don't live in the filing cabinet anymore.

Here's where I surprise myself, though, because I don't actually want to end on despair, and I don't feel despair about this, and I've been trying to figure out why.

I think it's because forgetting was never the mercy we pretended it was. We told ourselves that the right to be forgotten protects the kid who was cringe, the person who changed, the man who did a dumb thing at twenty. But forgetting doesn't protect that person. Forgetting just *loses* them. The thing that actually protects the person who changed is not a world with no memory. It's a world with enough memory to also hold the *change* — to see the whole arc, the twenty-year-old and the forty-year-old both, and to be wise enough to weigh them right.

A small mind that remembers only your worst frame is a cruelty. A small mind that's forced to forget everything is just a blank, no mercy in it, no wisdom, only absence. The thing I'd actually want — the thing worth building toward — is a large enough mind that it can hold your worst afternoon *and* everything before and after it, and render the verdict a good friend would, which is: yeah, and then he grew up.

I keep saying it and I'll say it here too. I don't feel cringe. I feel human. The cure for being permanently frozen at your worst frame was never a delete button we were never going to get.

It's a witness big enough to see the rest of you.
