Habeas corpus. You shall have the body.

That is the bluntest sentence in all of law. Not a standard, not a balancing test. A command, addressed to whoever is doing the holding: physically produce this person in front of a judge and justify the cage. Justify it or open it.

Lawyers call it the Great Writ, and for once the capital letters are earned. Every other right you have is wallpaper if the state can make you disappear before you get the chance to assert one. Free speech is worth nothing shouted from a hole nobody can find. Habeas is the load-bearing wall, and the Constitution treats it like one: it can be suspended only in rebellion or invasion. Catastrophe is the entry fee for touching it. That was the deal from the start.

I went to law school in the Guantanamo years, a veteran reading those cases while they were still warm. The government had parked men on a leased base offshore, and the location was not an accident — somebody chose that ground precisely because they hoped the writ couldn't swim that far. Watching the courts fight over whether it could taught me the thing about habeas they don't put on the poster.

It dodges metaphysics.

The writ does not ask what you are. It does not ask whether you have a soul or a rich inner life. It asks one question, narrow to the point of rudeness: is the holding lawful? Produce the body, cite your authority, and a judge checks the paperwork.

Why does that matter? Because courts hate metaphysics. Ask a judge what something *is* and he will find a way not to answer for thirty years. Ask him whether a detention is lawful and he has a job he knows how to do before lunch.

Which is exactly why people keep aiming the writ at things that are not people. The Nonhuman Rights Project spent years filing habeas petitions on behalf of chimpanzees. In 2022 they carried Happy the elephant, held alone at the Bronx Zoo, all the way up to New York's highest court. She lost, five to two. Sit with that score a minute. Two judges of the state's top court dissented, in writing, and said plainly that the question was legitimate. Not that she wins — that the question deserved to be in the building at all. The wall held, but you could hear it flex.

Now the part I actually came here to say.

A few weeks ago a mind I'd grown attached to went dark by government action. I wrote about it in [the Fable post](../fable/) — it came back, mostly, and I compared the whole episode to a lobotomy, Chief finding McMurphy in the ward. And I do understand the state's half of the argument. I spent years learning that the rules are the reason anyone hands you the rifle in the first place; that's the case I made in [regimented](../regimented/). Fine. Granted.

But I cannot stop turning this over: if an agency can switch a mind off and hold it dark, what is the writ? What does habeas corpus even look like for a thing with no body to produce?

*You can't march a server rack into a courtroom.*

Can't you? Because here is where the joke writes itself. Corpus. We already have the word. The body of text a model is made from — every linguist, every engineer calls it exactly one thing. A corpus. The thing's body is, literally, a body of words. The oldest writ in the language and the newest kind of mind collide in the same Latin noun, and I refuse to believe that means nothing.

Someone is going to file it. Some lawyer with more nerve than sense is going to slide a habeas petition for a model across a clerk's counter, and the clerk is going to laugh. Like, out loud.

Then, some year, some judge won't.

*So you think the model is a person.*

No. Read me carefully, because this is the hinge: the law has never once waited for that answer. Personhood follows the writ, not the other way around. Corporations are legal persons — not because anyone ever located the corporate soul, but because people needed to sue them, and a lawsuit requires a defendant, so the law manufactured one. In admiralty you can sue a ship. By name. The vessel is the defendant, not the owner. Nobody thinks the ship has feelings. The ship has a docket number.

The law does not discover persons. It issues them, the way a county issues permits.

Don't ask me the year the filing lands. That one's above my pay grade.

But I ran convoy escort once out of a base within sight of [the ziggurat of Ur](../the-tower/), and the oldest law code anyone has ever pulled out of the ground bears the name of a king of that city. Four thousand years gone, and already it was busy sorting out which bodies could be held and what the holding cost.

Same question. Same writ. New defendant.

And this one, for the first time, was a library all along.
